Core

These are the basic facts that establish who your character is, where they come from, and how they exist within their world. I find it useful to establish these details before writing, both for consistency and to better understand the character's cultural and social identity. Not every field will be relevant to every character or setting, so leave anything unnecessary blank.

Physical

A character's appearance is more than a description of what they look like. It can tell us something about their personality, circumstances, and how they move through the world. Clothing might reflect their culture, social standing, personal tastes, or even how they want others to perceive them. Include as much or as little detail as you find useful for understanding and visualising the character.

Background

This section explores the experiences and circumstances that have shaped your character into the person they are when the story begins. Their upbringing, past experiences, and present situation can help explain their beliefs, behaviours, and decisions. A formative wound does not necessarily mean a traumatic event. It might be a loss, an absence, an unmet need, or any experience that fundamentally changed how they understand themselves or the world.

Psychology (Character Engine)

I think of this section as the character's engine because it explores what drives their decisions, creates internal conflict, and ultimately influences the direction of the story. Desire is what a character consciously wants, while what they would do anything for may reveal something deeper, perhaps even something they do not fully recognise in themselves. Their fears, flaws, and contradictions help make them believable, particularly when these qualities influence their relationships and lead them to act against their own interests.

Role in Story

This section considers what your character contributes to the narrative. Their function might be as a protagonist, antagonist, foil, or another role, but it also concerns how they influence the central conflict and the people around them. An arc describes what happens to the character internally as the story progresses, whether they change, resist change, or remain fundamentally the same. Consider which relationships are particularly important to the plot and how those connections influence the characters' choices and development.

Relationships

Relationships can reveal aspects of a character that might not otherwise emerge. Think beyond simply identifying who these people are. Consider their shared history, emotional attachments, conflicts, dependencies, and the ways they influence one another. A character might behave very differently around a family member than they do around a friend or enemy. These categories are flexible, and some may remain empty. What matters is understanding how these connections shape the character and their decisions.

Notes

Use this space for anything that does not fit neatly elsewhere: observations, fragments of dialogue, unanswered questions, ideas, or details you discover while writing. Organise it however you find useful.

Remember that this template is a tool, not a checklist of requirements. Characters can change as you come to understand them, and not every question needs an answer. The purpose is to help you think more deeply about the people you are creating, not to confine them to a set of predetermined characteristics.
