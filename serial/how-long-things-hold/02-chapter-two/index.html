How Long Things Hold

Instalment II

Chapter Two

21 August 2026

Eîra couldn’t breathe. Her vision began to narrow as she clawed at Sâel’s wrists, but his grip only tightened further. The pressure was unrelenting, and she could feel her côr beginning to strain under the lack of aether.

“Sâ…el…” she choked out, his name barely a whisper.

His face was close to hers, his eyes wild and unseeing.

“A name,” he said, his voice almost tender. “Such a small thing. Such a small thing to refuse me.”

Eîra’s hand fumbled at her hip, her fingers closing around the hilt of her sword. Papa had given it to her, a practical gift.

For protection, my dear. One day, when I am gone, dangerous things may wander about. This will keep you safe.

She had never used it on anything living, and never dreamed of using it on another Vasentia. Her vision began to blur, dark spots appearing across her eyes as her côr pulsed weakly within her chest, faltering.

I’m going to die… he is going to kill me…

Her thoughts began to fragment and scatter as she looked up at Sâel's face, at the hunger and fury twisted together into something that had never learned to tell them apart.

This isn’t him… whatever it is… not… him anymore… The sword is… the only thing… the only way… just like Papa said… I’m sorry… I’m so… so sorry but…

Tears began to form in her eyes. “I am… sorry…” Eîra whispered.

She drew the blade out then drove it up through his chest. The resistance was brief as it slid through his flesh and bone, then broke through the crystalline seat of his animus with a sound like cracking glass. His hands released her throat immediately, his whole body going rigid. Eîra stumbled back, gasping, the sword still in her hand as it pulled from him with a wet sound.

The wildness left his eyes all at once. What replaced it was only Sâel: the confusion, the careful attempt to take stock, the slight furrow of his brow as he looked down at his chest, at the dark liquid spreading across his shirt. He looked back up at her.

“Eîra?” he gasped. His voice was his again, but small and bewildered.

What happened? Why does everything feel… so… far away?

His brow furrowed as he tried to make sense of the damage, to catalogue it the same way he catalogued everything else.

“I don’t— I don’t know what—” His legs gave out, and then he dropped to his knees, swaying. “Something is— something is wrong— with me.”

I am dying. The thought arrived in his mind with a strange clarity, distant and observational, like it belonged to someone else.

“I… am—”

His eyes searched her face once more, confused, pleading, trying to understand why she looked so frightened, why there were tears on her face, and a red mark across her throat. Then, the grey of his eyes dulled, and he fell forward onto the broken pavement.

Eîra stood frozen, the sword hanging loose in her hand, watching the dark liquid spread across his chest and pool onto the broken pavement beneath him. Virtric. The animus fluid that sustained a Vasentia’s physical form. His animus fluid. 

I pierced his côr, which means… I killed him… I killed him… I just… killed a…

She dropped the sword and grabbed Sâel’s ankles.

How long do I have? Hours… Minutes?

He was heavier than she had expected, and dragging him over the uneven pavement was agonising work. She pulled him a few feet, then stopped to catch her breath, and pulled again. The circlet above her head pulsed steadily above her, and each time she felt that she could go no further, it sent another wave of energy through her.

Papa had told her once, a long time ago, that if a Vasentia’s côr was damaged beyond self-repair, they had to be brought to a menhîrrum quickly, or they would be lost forever.

Just like the Dyn.

Just like Papa.

She pulled harder.

Please don’t die.

The ground scraped beneath him, the sound making her flinch.

I am sorry.

After a half hour, the library came into view. She had lived here for all this time, had walked the halls, slept in the archive rooms, sat in Papa’s chair and read from the books he loved before he had transferred them all to her. Now the books were dust. This was the place where he taught her to climb, to think for herself, and to remember. She could not let this place witness another loss.

She hauled Sâel up the steps, his coat catching on the cracked marble as she pulled him into the atrium. At the back of the room was the menhîrrum; matte black and featureless, except for the alcove carved into its surface. She dragged Sâel to it and stopped, her arms shaking with exhaustion. The circlet pulsed once more, then went still.

Eîra knelt beside his body and carefully lifted him, angling him into the alcove, positioning him as gently as she could, then folded his hands across his chest. His face was slack, and the light had gone from his eyes. She brushed her hand over his face, closing them, then placed her palm against the menhîrrum. She closed her eyes, turned her attention inward, and pushed.

The menhîrrum responded immediately, light blooming from within the alcove, soft and white, illuminating Sâel’s face from below. Eîra felt the connection open to the Source. The menhîrrum would restore Sâel to his last preservation. But he would wake with no memory of what had passed since the journey here, no memory of the survey assignment, no memory of meeting her. 

No memory of what she had done.

Your memories matter, my dear, Papa had said. Not just mine, not just the archive… You.

The light intensified, and Eîra pulled her hand back as the alcove sealed itself until it became a smooth surface. Inside, she knew it was working, rebuilding his côr from the pattern stored in the Source.

The restoration took three hours. In that time Eîra retrieved her sword from the street and cleaned it, her hands moving slowly over the blade, wiping away the virtric with a furrowed brow. She sat with her back against the wall, staring at the menhîrrum, watching the light slowly fade.

When the alcove finally opened, Sâel gasped and sat up. Eîra looked at him. There was no wound, but his shirt had a tear through the fabric, surrounded by the dark stain that had now dried stiff. Sâel looked down, his fingers tracing the tear in the fabric, then moved to his chest beneath.

He looked around the chamber, his eyes moving slowly over the ruined library, before finally settling on Eîra.

“Who are you?” he said, his voice hoarse. “Where am I?”

Eîra stood slowly. “I’m Eîra. You’re in my home,” she said quietly.

He climbed out of the alcove carefully, his movements still uncertain. “Your home?” He looked at the menhîrrum then back at her again. “I do not— I was in the settlement, waiting for my next survey assignment.” His brow furrowed. “What date is it?”

“Saturday,” Eîra said. “The tenth of January, the year fourty-eight o’four…”

He stopped, his hand moving unconsciously to his chest, and his eyes went out of focus briefly as he consulted an internal record. When they focused again, they were wide with confusion.

“That is… two days. I have lost two days.”

“You were… damaged,” Eîra said, keeping her voice level. “I found you nearby. Your côr had failed, so I brought you here.”

“Damaged?” He looked down at himself again. “How?”

“I don’t know,” Eîra said quickly. “You were already dead when I found you. Perhaps you fell on something.”

He stared at her, his mind working through the information, trying to catalogue the inconsistencies. “Where did you find me?”

“Near the overpass, about ten minutes from here.”

“You said you found me, but why were you there?”

“I walk through the ruins. I’ve lived here a long time, I know the area well.”

Sâel’s gaze moved over her face, then lingered at her throat. She resisted the urge to touch it, to cover the bruise she knew was there.

“Your neck. You are injured as well—” he began.

“Ah, it’s nothing. I fell while dragging you here. The pavement is uneven,” she said, cutting him off.

He didn’t seem convinced; his eyes tracked from her throat to the hole in his shirt, then across the room, where he saw her sword lying on a low table, still in its wooden scabbard. His gaze lingered for a moment.

She is small. The sword is light and narrow. She could not have— Could she?

He looked back at her face, at the careful blankness in her expression, then down again. His jaw tightened, but he said nothing.

Too many variables. Too much… confusion. He filed it away, then walked towards her. “You found me damaged, brought me to your menhîrrum, and restored me. Why?”

“Because you would have been lost otherwise.”

“That is not an answer. That is a justification. Why would you expend that effort on a stranger?”

Eîra met his gaze, her eyes steady despite the heaviness in her chest. “Because leaving you felt wrong.”

Sâel’s eyes searched her face, then he stepped back. “I need to return to the settlement. Report the data loss and request clarification on my assignment parameters.”

“Of course,” Eîra said quietly.

He moved towards the exit, then paused, looking back at her. “Thank you,” he said, the words stiff but genuine. “For the restoration.”

Eîra nodded, but did not speak.

He hesitated, then spoke again. “Your name… again?”

“Eîra,” she said quietly.

He nodded once, committing it to his memory. “I am Sâel.”

“I know—” she said, then caught herself. “I mea— you said it, um, earlier. When you first woke up.”

A slight furrow appeared between his brows, but he didn’t question her. “Right,” he said, then held her gaze for another moment before turning and walking out into the overgrown streets.

Eîra stood in the atrium and listened to his footsteps fade. When she was certain he was gone, she sank down against the wall again, her legs giving out. She looked over at the sword on the table. Clean and sheathed.

I killed him.

The thought kept coming back. She had watched him die because of what she carried, because she had been so desperate for connection that she had opened the archive without thinking. She had killed him, then brought him back, and then lied to him. And he remembered none of it. But she did; she would remember it forever, just as she remembered everything.

For all this time, she had been alone. She had hurt no one, harmed nothing. She had simply existed, a witness to the world’s slow reclamation. That version of herself felt impossibly distant now, like a character in one of the stories Papa used to read to her.

I am dangerous… the archive… it isn’t just books. It’s something else, something that can possess… and kill.

She pulled her knees to her chest and wrapped her arms around them, making herself small in the vast space of the library.

 

***

The next day the morning’s air was cool against Sâel’s face as he walked north. The sun had barely risen, casting long shadows across the ruins. He moved at his usual pace, the gap in his memory sitting in his consciousness, something his mind kept probing at, trying to find what should have been there.

He looked down as he walked. The tear in his shirt went straight through, just left of the centre of his chest.

The edges are clean, not ragged. A puncture. And the stain… definitely virtric. Something pierced my côr.

His hand moved to his chest, his fingers tracing the spot beneath the hole. The skin was intact, smooth, and there were no scars or marks. The menhîrrum had restored him completely.

It was not the loss itself that disturbed him. What disturbed Sâel was not knowing what he had lost.

The ruins gave way to a denser forest as he moved along. The road had disappeared in places, consumed by root systems and centuries of leaf litter. He stepped over a fallen trunk, its bark soft with rot, and continued on as birds called from the branches.

By the time the sun was low on the horizon, the settlement came into his view. It was located in what had once been a university campus, a cluster of brick buildings arranged around a central quad. Most of the structures were still intact enough to be functional. He walked closer.

 

***

Veyra had been watching the entrance since dawn, her hands moving at the task of making bread. Iren had noticed. He walked up, standing beside her at the table.

“He’s not due back for another few days,” he said. “But you keep watching.”

“I’m not watching,” Veyra said. “I’m working.”

Iren said nothing.

She folded the dough, looking down at it with a furrowed brow. “He goes out there alone. He always has. And he never asks for anything, and he never says when something is wrong, and one day—”

Iren put his hand on her shoulder, his face close to hers. “He’ll come back.”

“Yes… I know,” she said quietly.

Iren nodded, then kissed her cheek and walked back to his workshop.

When Sâel came through the gap in the wall, earlier than she had expected, Veyra stood up quickly, wiping her hands on a towel. Then she took a deep breath, walking towards him. His expression was the one he had when he was managing something. She recognised it. She had been watching him manage things for two hundred years.

“Sâel,” she said, approaching him with her usual unhurried grace.  She was tall, taller than him by nearly a head and a half, with a long brown braid that fell over one shoulder and pale grey-blue eyes that were doing a second pass over him before she had even stopped walking. When she did stop, she was close enough that he had to tilt his head back slightly to meet her gaze.

“Veyra,” he said quietly.

She looked at his face first. Then his hands. Then the hole in his shirt. She was quiet for a moment. “You’re back early. You said you’d be surveying the southern sector for another five days at least...”

“There was an incident. I lost two days of memory. So I have returned to make a report and get clarification on my assignment parameters.”

Her eyes went back to the shirt, to the dark stain around the puncture. She looked at it for a long moment, then back up at him. “Clarification on the assignment parameters…” she repeated flatly. “Sâel, you’re the only one who calls them that. The rest of us just say, ‘I went south, looked at some buildings.’”

“Documentation requires precision,” he said stiffly.

“Mmhm.” She tilted her head, studying him. “So then. What happened? You look intact to me. Well...” her gaze dropped back to his chest. “Mostly intact.”

“I was damaged, then restored at a menhîrrum in sector seven. The resident brought me there after finding me… non-functioning.”

“Restored? You mean you— Resident?”

“She identified herself as Eîra. She said it was her home, and there’s a menhîrrum in an old library structure.”

“Huh.” Veyra crossed her arms. “Is she pre-Departure, maybe?”

“Possibly. I didn’t confirm her age. I was...” He paused. “Disoriented after restoration. I didn’t conduct a proper assessment.”

“A proper assessment? Sâel, you woke up after being restored by a stranger. I think you’re allowed to be a little disoriented.”

His jaw tightened slightly. “I should have gathered more information.”

Veyra sighed. “Did she say what damaged you?”

“No. She said she found me unconscious, and my last memory was from here, before my departure. It is... suboptimal.”

Veyra looked at him for a long moment, then sighed. “Right. Well, since you’re so concerned about your ‘assignment parameters’ why don’t you take a few days to rest? Let things settle. The southern sector isn’t going anywhere.”

“I am functional. There’s no ne—”

“I’m not saying you’re not functional. I am saying that you lost two days, and you have been standing there with that look on your face since you came through the gate.”

“What look?”

“That one,” she said. “When you’re worried. Your eyes go somewhere else and the rest of you stays behind.”

Sâel looked away, then looked back, his jaw had loosened and his eyes had steadied. “I am not worried. I am trying to establish what happened so I can account for the gap accurately—”

Veyra reached out and tapped his forehead. “Take. A. Break.”

He looked up at her, then away towards the small building. “I should at least document what I can recall.”

“Of course you should. Just... maybe sleep first? Eat something? Do one thing that isn’t work?”

“Vasentia don’t need to ea—”

“I know we don’t need to, Sâel. That’s not the point.” She turned to walk away, then paused, looking over her shoulder. “And if you’re going to document this mystery resident and her menhîrrum, make sure to include the location. If she’s been there long and no one knew—”

“I was planning to include the coordinates.”

“Of course you were.” There was a warmth in her voice. “Welcome back, by the way. I am glad you’re intact. Truly.” She walked away before he could respond, her braid swaying slightly.

Sâel was almost to his building when someone else spoke.

“You’re back.”

The voice was lighter than Veyra’s, more animated. Sâel turned. Another Vasentia stood near the edge of the quad, one hand resting loosely at his side, the other adjusting the pale blue scarf draped across his shoulders. His amber eyes were fixed on Sâel with an intensity that always made him feel slightly uncomfortable.

“Lír,” Sâel said.

Lír tilted his head slightly, his gaze tracking downward to Sâel's chest and staying there. “Your shirt…” he said, taking a step closer. “It’s torn.”

Sâel glanced down at the hole, then back at Lír. “Yes. An incident… during the survey.”

“Hmm.” Lír moved closer, close enough that now Sâel could see the golden undertones in his hair catching the last of the sunlight. “That’s a puncture, clean edges… something narrow went through.” His fingers reached out, stopping just short of touching Sâel’s shirt. Then his eyes went distant and unfocused. “Lían says it looks like a blade wound.” Lír said quietly.

“Lían is… not here, Lír” Sâel replied.

“No.” Lír’s eyes refocused, meeting Sâel’s. “But he would say it anyhow. He was always better at noticing these things than I am.” Lír’s hand dropped back to his side, and he took a small step back, giving Sâel his space. “So then. You don’t remember what happened?”

“No,” Sâel replied.

“That must be—” Lír paused, his head tilting again. “Lían says it must be frightening to lose pieces of yourself and not know what they contained.”

“It is a known consequence of restoration,” Sâel said carefully. 

Lír smiled slightly, a sad, gentle expression that made him look even more fragile than usual. “You always say things like that.” He looked away, towards the horizon. “Lían thinks you’re afraid to say what you really feel.”

“I—”

“It’s alright,” Lír said, turning back, cutting him off. “I’m not criticising you. Just… ‘observing’.” His fingers moved to one of the wrapped fabric bands around his wrist, adjusting it absently. “You should clean the stain before it sets further. Virtric is quite difficult to remove once it oxidises.”

“Thank you,” Sâel said, uncertain what else to say.

Lír nodded once, then drifted away across the quad, his scarf trailing behind him.

Sâel continued towards the small building. The main hall was empty, the menhîrrum standing silent in the centre. He climbed the stairs to the second floor, where several rooms had been converted into private spaces. His was at the end of the hall, a small room with a single window overlooking the quad, a narrow bed he rarely used, and a desk where he kept his documentation materials.

He sat at the desk, grabbed a small black stone tablet, and focused his attention on it. He recorded what he could verify: the dates, the coordinates, the menhîrrum, her name. He left the rest as gaps.

He opened his eyes. There was more; the bruising on her neck, the sword, the look she had given him, like she knew something he didn’t. Her answers had been precise, cautious, each one arriving after a fraction of delay. He noted it all. But none of it was proof. Only inference. Only suspicion.

He set the tablet down and looked out the window. The quad was dark now, the last of the sunlight fading to deep blue.

Two days of his life were gone. Erased. It was the price of the system, the cost of enduring, but this felt different. He looked down at the torn fabric of his shirt, at the dark stain surrounding the hole.

What happened to me?

And beneath that question was another one, quieter, but more insistent.

What did I do?

 

***

The tree held Eîra’s weight the same way it always had. She sat in her usual branch, over the intersection, her legs angled to either side, hands resting on her thighs. The morning sun filtered through the canopy above.

Everything was the same, and yet nothing was the same.

She kept looking down at the street, half-expecting to see him walking through the intersection again, coming back with questions she couldn’t answer. He was a surveyor, thorough, methodical. He would return to complete his documentation, and this time he would ask harder questions. He might bring others with him who wouldn’t accept her answers. What if they came back suspicious? Or worse, angry?

Why did it happen?

She had carried the archive for over a millennium. Papa had said the books were important, that they mattered, that someone, anyone, would need to remember what the Dyn had created, had felt. They were hers to keep, hers to protect.

I should have known better… I should have been more careful… when he had asked… I said yes…

She had been so pleased that he wanted to see them, that someone was interested, that finally Papa’s dream could be realised.

I killed him… but I did it to save myself… I would do it again if I had to, but that doesn’t make it any easier.

Papa would have known what to do… he always knew what to do…

But Papa was gone, and had been gone for so long that sometimes she had trouble remembering what his face even looked like. His voice was perfectly preserved in her memory, every word he had ever said, exact and unchanging, but his face was like writing left out in the rain.

She could recall the shape of it, the colour of his eyes, brown like earth, and kind. The grey streaks in his hair and the lines around his mouth when he smiled. But when she tried to picture it all together, it was like a blur. All she had was a collection of details, a catalogue rather than a memory.

You are my memory, Eîra… my dearest memory… so promise me… that you will remember… do not forget.

She had promised, of course she had promised, and she had kept it. For eleven hundred and seventy-five years, she had remembered everything Papa had given her. Every book, every word he’d said, every thing she had seen in this clearing.

But he hadn’t told her what to do if the stories broke people, if they became dangerous. What to do if someone touched the archive, and it touched them back.

Did Papa know this could happen?

She looked down at the street again. It was empty and quiet, the same as it always had been.

Perhaps I should find him… I could find his settlement, and… Do what? Apologise? Explain? Tell him what really happened? No… it’s better he doesn’t know… and never comes back… better to forget me altogether…

She would stay here, in her tree, in her clearing, with her archive, with her memories, and her guilt.

She sat there as the morning stretched into afternoon, the light shifting from gold to white and then back to gold again. She watched the street below, watched the shadows move across the broken pavement, watched the small creatures scurry about.

Finally, as the sun began to sink below the horizon, she stood up carefully on the branch and then descended the tree in silence, landing lightly on the street below. The library waited, dark, empty, and familiar. The menhîrrum stood in the atrium, black and smooth and silent. She didn’t approach it. She had already preserved herself yesterday, committing everything to the Source. There was nothing new to add. Only the same memories, playing over and over.

She walked past the menhîrrum to Papa’s chair and sank into it. It was just rusted metal now, the old padding and fabric completely gone. 

What is the archive really? What is inside of me?

All Eîra knew was that the archive was dangerous, and by extension, that meant that she was dangerous. That sharing it carried risks she hadn’t anticipated. That Papa’s gift, this beautiful, precious thing, might be meant to stay locked away forever. 

Which means I will have to stay locked away forever.

The silence of the library settled, heavier than it had ever been before. 

For a thousand years that silence had been her companion. 

Now, it was just silence.
